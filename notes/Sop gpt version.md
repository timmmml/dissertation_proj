My interest stems from my interdisciplinary background in cognitive and computational neuroscience and information engineering. Through my past research experiences, I developed a vision of the brain as a dynamical information-processing system that orchestrates behavior across multiple skills, and I am excited to further investigate this vision in my PhD studies. 

After my first summer internship with Prof. Paul Bays at Cambridge studying neural constraints at working memory and perceptual limits using psychophysics methods, I got intrigued by prospects of computationally modelling behaviors to capture how the brain processes environmental infromation. This motivated me to acquire the competitive Experimental Psychology Society Bursary to work with Prof. Patricia Lockwood and Dr. Paul Muhle-Karbe in Birmingham, where I was able to bring psychophysics tools to study the interplay between metacognition and mentalization in dynamical social decision making. By developing cognitive models that capture how the brain's representation of task variables update through trials, I realized the importance of the dynamical nature of this system. This realization pointed my interests to recapitulating the dynamics of cognitive processes with neural models, and led to a current collaboration with Dr. Marcelo Mattar, where I aim to develop a method to use interpretable recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to model cognitive dynamics from choices and other behavioral variables such as confidence and reaction times in decision making tasks. 

Taking intensive coursework on neurobiology, computational neuroscience and brain machine interfaces (BMIs), I also realized that the family of cognitive models I was building only describe the brain's responses in abstract terms. Cognitive models try to capture the brain's dynamics across trials in order to study how it algorithmically solve cognitive tasks, without considering the underlying neural mechanisms. 
On the other hand, BMI applications explicitly zoom in to the neural level, trying to capture the brain's dynamics within trials by statistically inferring the evolution of latent factors encoded by neural population activities, often on "neural manifolds" that encode behaviorally relevant variables.

To me, the neural resolution in modelling the brain's dynamics is crucial. It represents a common ground for cognitive research and other branches of neuroscience such as motor control and sensory processing. This motivated me to acquire a competitive grant from Mathworks to work with Prof. Guillaume Hennequin on neural representations of motor plans and state variables. With Guillaume's expertise in control theory, neural networks, and computational investigation of motor control, I investigated how action plans in non-Euclidean spaces such as the space of 3D rotations are represented in my novelly designed predictive-control-based architecture, and how it changes with different constraints on the neural system. In order to investigate the full loop of motor control, I am currently working with Guillaume on my dissertation project which focuses on neural representations of state variables within the highly successful continuous attractor network (CAN) paradigm. 

Through works with Guillaume in the realm of motor control, I realized that the dynamical information processing in the brain's motor system can be described as that of a series of chained controllers, and this hierarchical view is key to bringing cognitive models to neural precision. Namely, decision making in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), often the target of cognitive models, sits at the top of this hierarchy, controlling downstream regions such as the premotor cortex (PMC) and motor cortex (M1), which in turn control spinal circuits. Naturalistic behavior reflects seamless orchestration of local dynamics and global communication across system.  

In my intended PhD studies, I aim to investigate how behavior is planned and controlled across the hiearchy of brain regions. Two key components underlie this ambition: (1) understanding how the brain's factorization of computations map to neural representations in different brain regions, and (2) how these regions communicate to produce coherent and flexible behavior.

Princeton’s Neuroscience PhD program, particularly its Quantitative and Computational Neuroscience track, is uniquely suited to my ambitions. The structured computational training will enable me to bridge theoretical concepts and empirical data, and Princeton's tight community of leading researchers provide an ideal setting for me to contribute to the studies of neural dynamics at multiple scales. Specifically, I am interested in working with Prof. Tim Buschman and Dr. Tatiana Engel. 

Prof. Buschman's research on inter-areal control across neural populations and cognitive control is directly relevant for my research goals, and his combined use of calcium imaging, electrophysiology, and neuroimaging offers an intriguing framework to study flexible behavior across model systems. My experience with experimental research and cognitive modelling would be directly relevant in his research on cognitive control, and my work on neural representations of states and actions would be add a valuable perspective in his research on inter-areal control and brain dynamics.

On the other hand, Dr. Engel's work identifying circuit mechanisms from large-scale neural recordings offers crucial methodological insights for this research. Specifically, her work on neural manifolds, neural circuits, and neural dynamics offers a valuable way to bridge anatomical and electrophysiological datasets to mechanistic circuit models such as CANs. This would be invaluable in my research on bridging principles of inter-areal neural communication to powerful neural dynamics models that operate on multi-area neural data. Having training from information engineering with a focus on machine learning, information theory, and control, I am excited about the possibility of engaging with her research and contributing to the development of novel methods to study neural dynamics. 

Looking ahead, I see this PhD as pivotal for advancing my understanding of hierarchical brain functions. Building on Princeton's strong computational neuroscience foundation and its network of researchers, I aim to stay in academia and continue to bridge together different methods of neuroscientific enquiries. In addition, I will be thrilled to be involved in translating neuroscientific insights to practical applications that can improve the quality of life of those in need. First, I envision my research to lead to principled ways of handling multi-area neural recordings, which could be crucial for developing next-generation BMIs. On the other hand, this type of insights could guide the design of synthetic systems of brain dynamics, which could be used to test network-level hypotheses for psychiatric symptoms and to iterate and discover novel modes of treatments.
