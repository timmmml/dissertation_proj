My interest stems from my interdisciplinary background in cognitive and computational neuroscience and information engineering. Through research and coursework, I developed a vision of the brain as a dynamical information-processing system. Cognitive models capture the brain’s responses to stimuli in decision-making tasks, while neural models in motor control often target latent factors in neural recordings, as in Brain Machine Interface (BMI) applications. Both aim to capture brain dynamics by using descriptive statistical methods to model observed data (choice/neural recording), but cognitive models typically address computations over seconds, while neural models focus on signals at the millisecond scale.

I believe this separation of time scales is largely an artifact of experimental design: naturalistic behavior unfolds continuously, not separated by trials. For example, in collaborative predation, an animal integrates social hierarchy, spatial navigation, and precise motor control. Each computation has been studied in isolation, often linked to different brain regions: decision-making in the prefrontal cortex (PFC), spatial representation in frontal-hippocampal interactions, and motor planning in the premotor cortex (PMC) and motor cortex (M1), with sensory feedback from parietal regions. These computations form a hierarchical structure in which upstream computations specify the context of downstream ones, extending down into subcortical structures and spinal circuits. 
Naturalistic behavior reflects seamless orchestration across this system.

In my intended career as a researcher, I aim to bridge the gap implied by the separation of time scales by investigating how behavior is planned and controlled across the hiearchy of brain regions. Two key components underlie this ambition: (1) understanding how the brain's factorization of computations map to neural representations in different brain regions, and (2) how these regions communicate to produce coherent and flexible behavior. 

Characterizing this neural computation flow has clear practical impacts. First, it enables us to create interpretable synthetic systems for clinically important scenarios. Controlled perturbations on these systems would allow psychiatric researchers to form/test network-level hypotheses for psychiatric symptoms and to develop technology that optimize personalized treatments based on patients' neurobehavioral profiles. Additionally, this understanding can enhance robust and flexible action intention decoding in next-generation BMIs by combining recordings from different brain areas in a principled way.

Achieving this goal requires interdisciplinary insights that integrate nonhuman model systems, human neuroimaging, and artificial systems. Princeton University's Neuroscience PhD program offers a unique opportunity to engage with leading researchers whose work span these areas and whose interests align with my vision. In addition, the program's Quantitative and Computational Neuroscience track provides a structured environment to develop the computational skills necessary to bridge the gap between theoretical concepts and empirical validation. 

First, Prof. Tim Buschman's research aligns with my interest in understanding inter-areal control across neural populations in the brain and the brain's representation and manipulation of cognitive variables.
Specifically, his lab uses a combination of experimental methods, spanning from advanced cortex-wide calcium imaging and electrophysiological recordings in mice to human neuroimaging and decoding. This combination of methods offers a unique opportunity to engage with the full spectrum of model systems to holistically understand the brain's computations.

In addition,  Dr. Tatiana Engel's expertise in developing computational methods in identifying circuit mechanisms from neural recordings is particularly relevant for my desired level of understanding. Her work on methods that capture the dynamics and tuning in large-scale neural recordings is crucial for bridging neural data with novel theories of how these computations are implemented mechanistically, and I would be thrilled to engage with this research.
