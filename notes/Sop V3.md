My passion for understanding the brain as a dynamic, information-processing system is shaped by my interdisciplinary background in cognitive neuroscience, computational neuroscience, and information engineering. This holistic perspective has driven me to explore the brain's orchestration of behavior across time scales, and I am eager to further investigate these ideas through graduate studies.

My journey began with a profound interest in computational modeling of behavior, highlighting the importance of dynamical systems in understanding brain function. During my internship with Prof. Paul Bays at Cambridge, I studied neural constraints in working memory and perceptual limits using psychophysics methods. This experience sparked my curiosity about using computational models to capture how the brain processes environmental information, and I secured the Experimental Psychology Society Bursary to work with Prof. Patricia Lockwood and Dr. Paul Muhle-Karbe in Birmingham to pursue this interest. There, I applied psychophysical tools to study the interplay between metacognition and mentalization in dynamic social decision-making.
This experience in developing cognitive models to capture cognitive variables' evolution through trials underscored the brain's dynamical nature, leading to my current collaboration with Dr. Marcelo Mattar where I develop interpretable recurrent neural networks methods to model cognitive dynamics from multimodal behavioral data.

With these experiences, I realized that cognitive models often abstract away from the underlying neural mechanisms and sought to bridge this gap. While cognitive models capture behavior across trials and can be linked to prefrontal cortex (PFC) activation via neuroimaging datasets, the time scale is coarse and within-trial neural dynamics may be overlooked. Brain-machine interface (BMI) applications focus precisely on within-trial dynamics, inferring the evolution of latent factors encoded by neural populations often in the motor cortex (M1).

In light of the field's increasing ability to record from multiple brain regions simultaneously, I am motivated to extend BMI models' neural resolution to complement inter-trial cognitive models in more complex behavior. The hierarchical structure of the motor system presents a natural recipe for this goal, as cognitive decision-making (PFC) and motor control (premotor cortex-M1) can be viewed as two ends of a series of chained controllers. 

To elucidate neural dynamics across the hierarchy, my first step has focused on understanding neural circuits' representations of different state and action variables, which can often live on non-Euclidean manifolds, with a competitive grant from MathWorks to work with Prof. Guillaume Hennequin 
Through the summer, I designed a novel neural network architecture for motor planning in SO(3) and investigated how neural constraints affect action representations. In my current dissertation, I continue off by utilizing the continuous attractor network (CAN) framework to study neural circuitry that maintains state representations. By developing methods to train CANs for diverse manifolds and analyzing neural control and computation with these state/action representations, I aim to extend CAN's mechanistic framework into more complex action repertoires.

In my next steps, I wish to travel upward in the motor hierarchy to investigate neural processes underlying cognitively controlled behavior. My goals are twofold: first, to understand how the brain decomposes naturalistic behavioral control into neural representations among populations along the hierarchy; second, to elucidate how these populations communicate to produce coherent and flexible behavior. 

For this goal, I plan to use a combination of experimental and theoretical methods. I am confident that Princeton's PhD program in Neuroscience will provide the ideal platform, where I can engage with a community of leading researchers and immerse myself in rigorous empirical and computational training (core+QCN track). I am particularly excited about collaborating with faculty who share my interests, such as Prof. Tim Buschman and Dr. Tatiana Engel.

Working with Prof. Tim Buschman would allow me to explore inter-areal control and cognitive processes via a combination of experimentation and quantitative modelling. Having the fortune to chat with him in person and on Zoom, I find his research on inter-areal interactions and cognitive control an ideal fit for my interests. These questions are uniquely answered with his investigations across model systems, from mice to humans, with integrated usage of calcium imaging, electrophysiology, and neuroimaging (e.g., Tafazoli et al. and MacDowell et al., both in bioRxiv). My experience with experimental design and cognitive modeling would be directly applicable to his work on cognitive control, and my focus on neural representations of states and actions could add valuable perspectives to his research on inter-areal control and brain dynamics.

Collaborating with Dr. Tatiana Engel would additionally provide crucial theoretical and methodological insights into neural dynamics and circuit mechanisms. I am first drawn to her lab by a recent perspective that bridges neural manifolds, circuit models, and neural architectures (Langdon, Genkin, & Engel, 2023). This perspective, along with her methodological research such as the Predictive VAE (Wang, Tsin, & Engel, 2023), can potentially bridge anatomical and electrophysiological datasets with mechanistic models such as CANs. This aligns well with my interest in mechanistically investigating neural dynamics across brain areas, and I am eager to contribute to her research developing powerful methods that operate on multi-area neural recordings with my background in information engineering, machine learning, information theory, and control. 

Beyond my research, I am committed to contributing to the academic community through collaboration, mentorship, and the exchange of ideas. My interdisciplinary background positions me to bridge different neuroscientific and technological methodologies, fostering connections across fields. 

After completing my PhD, I plan to continue in academia, advancing research that integrates cognitive and neural perspectives. 
Ultimately, I am eager to translate neuroscientific insights into practical applications that improve the quality of life of those in need. Developing principled methods for handling multi-area neural recordings could be crucial for next-generation brain-machine interfaces, and understanding dynamical computations across hierarchical brain structures could guide the creation of synthetic systems to test network-level hypotheses for psychiatric conditions. These directions are closely related to Prof. Buschman's innovative work on precision brain stimulation and Dr. Engel's exciting involvement in the International Brain Laboratory's initiative mapping computations across the brain, and I would be therefore thrilled to collaborating with them on these fronts.

I am enthusiastic about the opportunity to contribute to the field of neuroscience. My experiences have prepared me to take on the challenges of graduate study, and I am eager to collaborate with others to unravel the complexities of the brain.
