My interdisciplinary background in cognitive and computational neuroscience and information engineering has shaped my vision of the brain as a dynamical information-processing system orchestrating behavior across multiple skills. I am eager to further investigate this vision in my PhD studies in Princeton's Neuroscience PhD program, for both its structured computational training and its tight community of leading researchers whose research aligns with my interest, such as Prof. Tim Buschman and Dr. Tatiana Engel.

My initial research experiences introduced me to computational modeling of behavior and highlighted the importance of dynamical systems in understanding brain function. During my first summer internship with Prof. Paul Bays at Cambridge, I studied neural constraints in working memory and perceptual limits using psychophysics methods. This work intrigued me about computationally modeling behaviors to capture how the brain processes environmental information. Motivated by this interest, I secured the competitive Experimental Psychology Society Bursary to work with Prof. Patricia Lockwood and Dr. Paul Muhle-Karbe in Birmingham. There, I applied psychophysics tools to study the interplay between metacognition and mentalization in dynamical social decision-making. By developing cognitive models that capture how the brain's representation of task variables updates through trials, I realized the significance of the system's dynamical nature. This realization directed my interests toward recapitulating cognitive dynamics with neural models, leading to my current collaboration with Dr. Marcelo Mattar where I aim to develop methods using interpretable recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to model cognitive dynamics from choices and behavioral variables like confidence and reaction times.

Through intensive coursework in neurobiology, computational neuroscience, and brain-machine interfaces (BMIs), I realized that while cognitive models describe the brain's responses in abstract terms across trials, they often overlook the underlying neural mechanisms. Conversely, BMI applications focus explicitly on the neural level, capturing within-trial dynamics by inferring the evolution of latent factors encoded by neural population activities on "neural manifolds" that represent behaviorally relevant variables.

Driven by the importance of neural resolution in modeling brain dynamics, I pursued research that integrates cognitive and neural perspectives. The motor system is a prime model system for this integration, as it presents a hierarchical structure that connects the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the motor cortex (M1), respectively targets of traditional cognitive and neural dynamics models. Information flow in this system can be described as a series of chained controllers, where decision-making processes in PFC control intermediate motor planning processes in the premotor cortex (PMC), which in turn control M1 and then spinal circuits. Naturalistic behavior reflects the seamless orchestration of local dynamics and global communication across this system.

Neural populations across different stages of the hierarchy encode variables that naturally live on non-Euclidean manifolds, such as 3D rotations in SO(3). To study how neural circuits represent these variables in various spaces, I obtained a competitive grant from MathWorks to work with Prof. Guillaume Hennequin to investigate signatures of neural representatinos of these.

Leveraging Guillaume's expertise in control theory, neural networks, and computational neuroscience, I desigend a novel neural network architecture to perform motor planning in SO(3) and investigated the role of different constraints in the acquired action representations.  In my current dissertation project, I leverage the continuous attractor network (CAN) framework to further study neural circuitry that hold state representations, by developing a method to train CANs for diverse manifolds and analyzing neural control and computation based on these networks.

In my PhD studies, I aim to further investigate how behavior is planned and controlled across the motor hierarchy with a mixture of experimental and theoretical methods. 
Two key components underpin this ambition: (1) understanding how the brain's factorization of computations maps to neural populations in different regions, and (2) elucidating how these regions communicate to produce coherent and flexible behavior.

Princeton’s Neuroscience PhD program is uniquely suited to these ambitions. With the Quantitative and Computational Neuroscience track, the program's structured training and coursework will enable me all the tools for combining empirical approaches with computational methods. In addition, Princeton's tight-knit community of leading researchers provides an ideal environment for me to contribute to research that follows my interested direction. Specifically, I am interested in working with Prof. Tim Buschman and Dr. Tatiana Engel.

Working with Prof. Tim Buschman would allow me to explore inter-areal control and cognitive processes via a combination of experimentation and quantitative modelling. His research on neural populations and cognitive control is particularly relevant, and his combined use of calcium imaging, electrophysiology, and neuroimaging offers an intriguing framework to study flexible behavior across model systems. My experience with experimental design and cognitive modeling would be directly applicable to his work on cognitive control, and my focus on neural representations of states and actions could add valuable perspectives to his research on inter-areal control and brain dynamics.

Collaborating with Dr. Tatiana Engel would additionally provide crucial theoretical and methodological insights into neural dynamics and circuit mechanisms. Her work on neural manifolds, circuits, and dynamics bridges anatomical and electrophysiological datasets with mechanistic circuits where models such as CANs are useful. This approach would be invaluable for my research on inter-areal neural communication and I am eager to contribute to her research developing powerful neural dynamics models that operate on multi-area neural data with my background in information engineering, machine learning, information theory, and control.

Building on Princeton's strong computational neuroscience foundation and its network of researchers, I plan to stay in academia following the PhD and continue bridging different neuroscientific methodologies. Additionally, I am eager to translate neuroscientific insights into practical applications that can improve the quality of life for those in need. For instance, I am excited to develop principled methods of handling multi-area neural recordings in developing next-generation brain-machine interfaces (BMIs). Furthermore, insights in the dynamical computations across the hierarchical structures in the fore brain could guide approaches creating synthetic systems where network-level hypotheses can be discovered and tested for psychiatric symptoms and to discover novel treatment modalities.
